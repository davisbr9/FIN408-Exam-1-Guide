Deck 1: Why Are FIs Special (26 slides)

Why financial intermediaries are special

Objectives from slide 2: the special role FIs play, why they get special regulation, what makes some FIs more special than others, and the crisis in financial markets.

Without FIs vs with FIs (slides 3 to 5)

Without FIs (slide 3): households (net savers) would have to buy equity and debt claims directly from corporations (net borrowers), handing over cash straight to the firm.

Why that flow of funds is low (slide 4): three costs get in the way, and FIs exist to cut them down.

Monitoring costs
Economies of scale let an FI screen and monitor borrowers cheaper than any one household could.
Liquidity costs
Direct claims on a firm are hard to turn into cash quickly.
Price risk
Selling a direct claim on the secondary market risks a bad price.

With FIs (slide 5): households give cash to the FI and get back deposits or insurance policies. The FI, acting as broker or asset transformer, gives cash to corporations and gets back equity and debt claims.

Functions of FIs (slide 6)

Brokerage function
Acts as an agent for the saver, providing information and transaction services. Produces economies of scale.
Asset transformation
Issues its own, more attractive financial claims (deposits, insurance policies) to household savers, and uses that money to buy primary securities from corporations.

Role of FIs in cost reduction (slide 7)

  • Information costs: without a monitor, investors are exposed to agency costs.
  • FI as delegated monitor: the FI has a bigger incentive to monitor than any single saver, and gets economies of scale in obtaining information.
  • FI as information producer: new secondary securities (like bank loans) can let FIs monitor more effectively, and short-term contracts give FIs more control and monitoring power.
  • Net result: reduction of information asymmetry between savers and borrowers.

Liquidity, price risk, and diversification (slide 8)

  • Secondary claims issued by FIs carry less price risk.
  • Demand deposits and similar claims are more liquid, which makes them more attractive to small investors.
  • FIs have an advantage over households in their ability to diversify, simply because of their size.
Note from the slide

The S&L debacle of the 1980s was linked to inadequate diversification of savings and loans, especially in the oil-based economies of the Southwest.

Other special services (slide 9)

Reduced transaction costs
Economies of scale on buying and selling assets.
Maturity intermediation
Bridging savers who want short-term claims with borrowers who want long-term loans.
Transmission of monetary policy
Banks carry Fed actions to the rest of the economy.
Credit allocation
Directing funds toward areas of special need, like home mortgages.
Intergenerational transfers (time intermediation)
Moving wealth across generations.
Payment services
Fedwire and CHIPS.
Denomination intermediation
Letting small investors own pieces of large-denomination assets.
Notes from the slide

Agriculture, small businesses, and home ownership have gotten special treatment through loan subsidies, guarantees, and other liquidity-improving activities. A key reason non-FIs haven't been allowed to act as banks is the fear of letting them access the payment system; protecting that system is paramount for regulators.

Specialness and regulation (slides 10 to 11)

FIs get special regulatory attention because of the negative externalities of FI failure, the special services FIs provide, and institution-specific functions: money supply transmission (banks), credit allocation (thrifts, farm banks), payment services (banks, thrifts).

Six types of regulation exist to enhance the net social welfare benefits of FI services:

Safety and soundness regulation (slides 12 to 13)

  • Diversification of assets: no more than 15 percent of equity to a single borrower.
  • Minimum capital requirements: TARP and the Capital Purchase Program (CPP).
  • Guaranty funds: the Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF) and the Securities Investors Protection Corporation (SIPC).
  • Monitoring and surveillance: on-site exams and review of accounting statements. The FDIC monitors and regulates DIF participants. Scrutiny increases after crises.
  • Regulation is not costless: this is the net regulatory burden.

Monetary policy regulation (slide 14)

Banks transmit monetary policy from the Fed to the rest of the economy. The Fed directly controls outside money (notes and coins). The bulk of the money supply is inside money (deposits), and banks may be required to hold a specified level of cash reserves against deposits.

Credit allocation regulation (slide 15)

Supports socially important sectors like housing and farming, through requirements for minimum amounts of assets in a sector, or maximum interest rates or fees to subsidize it. Examples: the Qualified Thrift Lender (QTL) test (65 percent of assets in residential mortgages), usury laws, and Regulation Q (now abolished).

Consumer protection regulation (slides 16 to 17)

Prevents discrimination in lending: the Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) and the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA). The FFIEC processed information on as many as 7.3 million mortgage transactions in 2017. Analysts question the net benefit versus the burden. Possible extensions: applying the CRA to other FIs, like insurers, given consolidation and universal banking. Examples: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (2010) and the credit card reform bill effective 2010.

Notes from the slide

The CRA has been widely criticized as imposing a greater regulatory burden than the sum of its social benefits. Hedge funds have come under much greater scrutiny since the Long Term Capital Management crisis.

Investor protection regulation (slide 18)

Aimed at protecting investors who use investment banks directly or indirectly, against abuses like insider trading, lack of disclosure, malfeasance, and breach of fiduciary responsibilities. Key legislation: the Securities Acts of 1933 and 1934, the Investment Company Act of 1940, and the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010.

Entry regulation (slide 19)

The entry and activities of FIs are regulated. The level of entry barriers affects profitability and the value of an FI's charter. Regulations define the scope of permitted activities: the Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999 affects charter value and the size of the net regulatory burden.

Note from the slide

Rules on the scope of activities vary widely by country. New Zealand has historically had very few restrictions. Most countries are reluctant to let industrial and banking activities mix, given the risks to the banking system and the potential for cross-subsidizing industrial activities with the bank.

Changing dynamics of specialness (slides 20 to 21)

Trends in the U.S.: a decline in the share of depository institutions and insurance companies, and increases in investment companies. This may reflect the net regulatory burden on depository FIs, savers preferring the denomination intermediation that mutual funds provide, and the Financial Services Modernization Act.

Risk and the financial crisis (slide 22)

Reactions to the FSM Act and other factors:

  • A shift from "originate and hold" to "originate and distribute," which changed incentives to monitor and control risk.
  • A shift to off-balance-sheet risks, which degraded quality and increased risk.
  • The housing market bubble encouraged the subprime market and more exotic mortgages.

Enterprise risk management (slide 23)

ERM recognizes the value of prioritizing and managing risks on an interrelated portfolio basis, rather than one at a time. The recent crisis pushed this into focus. Before the crisis, the emphasis was on processes and systems; after, attention shifted to building a strong risk culture supported by governance arrangements.

FinTech (slide 24)

Financial technology, or FinTech, means using technology to deliver financial solutions in a way that competes with traditional financial methods. Services include cryptocurrencies (like bitcoin) and blockchain.

Global trends (slide 25)

U.S. FIs face increased competition from foreign FIs. Only 2 of the top ten banks are U.S. banks. Foreign bank assets in the U.S. are typically more than 10 percent of the total, and as high as 21.9 percent.

Specialness of depository FIs (slides 6 to 7)

Depository FIs offer products on both sides of the balance sheet: loans on the asset side, deposits on the liabilities side. That's what sets them apart from other FI types.

Other outputs of depository FIs (slide 8)

In 1950: payment services, savings products, fiduciary services. By 2016, that expanded to frequently include underwriting of debt and equity, plus insurance and risk management products.

Size of depository FIs (slides 9 to 10)

Consolidation has created some very large FIs, from the combined effects of disintermediation, global competition, regulatory changes, technological developments, and competition across different FI types.

Commercial banks (slide 11)

The largest group of depository institutions. They differ from other FIs in the composition of assets and liabilities, and in regulatory oversight. Large and small commercial banks differ in structure and composition: larger banks make more commercial and industrial loans, small banks make more real estate loans. It's a mix of very large banks with very small banks.

Note from the slide

Commercial banks accept deposits and make consumer, commercial, and real estate loans.

Structure and composition (slides 12 to 13)

The number of banks is shrinking: 14,416 commercial banks in 1985, 12,744 in 1989, 4,774 in 2018. This is mostly the result of mergers and acquisitions, which were prevented prior to the 1980s and 1990s. Consolidation has reduced the asset share of the smallest banks (under $1 billion).

Chart: changes in number of banks, 1980 to 2021

Three lines: mergers, new charters, and failed banks. Mergers spiked in the late 1980s and again in the mid-1990s to 2000s, then trended down. New charters were strong through the 1980s and 1990s, then collapsed to near zero after 2008. Failed banks spiked around 2009 to 2011 (the financial crisis) after being near zero for most of the 1990s and 2000s.

Regulation, functions, and structure (slide 14)

Structural changes in banking generally result from changes in regulatory policy. Example: the change permitting interstate branching, the Riegle-Neal Act of 1994.

Structure and composition of commercial banks (slide 17)

Limited powers to underwrite corporate securities have existed only since 1987. The Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999 permitted commercial banks, investment banks, and insurance companies to merge.

Composition of the commercial banking sector (slide 18)

Community banks
Small, local.
Regional or superregional banks
Have access to the federal funds market to finance their lending and investment activities.
Money center banks
Bank of New York Mellon, Deutsche Bank (Bankers Trust), Citigroup, J.P. Morgan Chase, HSBC Bank USA.

Balance sheet and trends (slide 19)

Key trend since 1987: business loans have declined in importance while securities and mortgages have increased. What's driving this?

  • The increased importance of alternative funding through the commercial paper market.
  • Securitization of mortgage loans.
  • Temporary effects: credit crunches during the recessions of 1989 to 1992 and 2001 to 2002.
Chart: portfolio shift, 1984 to 2021 (Figure 2-4)

Real estate loans rose from about 30% of assets to a mid-2000s peak near 38%, dipped after the crisis, then climbed again after 2020. Securities bounced between 15% and 24%. Commercial and industrial loans slid from around 16% down toward 8 to 10%. Cash assets fell from about 8% to near 4% before jumping after 2020. Loans to individuals stayed fairly flat, around 7 to 10%.

Reading this table
  • 2009 is the only year with negative ROA and ROE (−0.10% and −0.93%). That's the financial crisis.

  • The number of institutions fell every single year shown here, from 12,709 (1989) to 4,231 (2021).

Deck 4: Bank Regulation (10 slides)

Bank regulation

Who regulates which bank, how bank failures have run since 1934, and the 13 laws named on the legislation slides.

Key regulatory agencies (slide 3)

The FDIC
Runs the Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF). Its role is preventing contagious "runs" or panics.
The OCC
Its primary function is to charter (and close) national banks.
The FRS (Federal Reserve)
Monetary policy and lender of last resort. National banks are automatically FRS members; state-chartered banks can elect to join.
State authorities
Perform functions similar to the OCC's, for state-chartered banks.
Dual banking system
The coexistence of national and state-chartered banks.
