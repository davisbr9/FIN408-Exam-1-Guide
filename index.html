The core ROE formula chain

ROE = ROA × EM = PM × AU × EM
NIE = NIA × AE = NITOI × TOIA × AE
Each piece cancels to confirm the identity: (NI/A)(A/E) = NI/E, and (NI/TOI)(TOI/A) = NI/A.

  • A = Total assets
  • E = Total equity capital
  • TOI = Total operating income = Interest income + Noninterest income

CLEAREDspecialness

From the slides: Why Are FIs Special? (Ch 1 deck). Support: Ch 1 end-of-chapter questions 1 to 32

Why financial intermediaries are special

Picture a world with no banks: every household has to find, vet, and babysit its own borrowers. FIs exist because that world is expensive.

Without FIs, money barely flows

Households (net savers) would buy corporations' (net borrowers) debt and equity directly. Three problems keep that flow small:

Monitoring costs
Checking up on a borrower is expensive for one small investor. FIs get economies of scale in screening and monitoring.
Liquidity costs
A direct claim on a company is hard to turn into cash fast.
Price risk
You might have to sell below what you paid.
Ch 1 question 1: risks common to all FIs

Credit (default) risk on assets, interest rate risk from maturity mismatches, liquidity (withdrawal) risk, underwriting risk, and operating risk.

Two functions

Brokerage functionAsset transformation
What it doesActs as an agent for the saver, providing information and transaction servicesIssues its own claims (deposits, insurance policies) to savers, then uses the money to buy primary securities (corporate debt and equity)
Key phraseEconomies of scaleSecondary claims are more attractive than primary claims
ExampleA stockbroker placing your tradeA bank turning your deposit into a business loan

How FIs cut costs

  • Information costs and agency costs: investors are exposed to managers acting against them.
  • Delegated monitor: savers hand monitoring to the FI, which has a bigger incentive to do it because it holds a big stake.
  • Information producer: economies of scale in gathering information.
  • Better contracts: new secondary securities like bank loans, and short-term contracts that give the FI more control.
  • All of this reduces information asymmetry.

Liquidity and price risk: FI claims (like demand deposits) are more liquid and carry less price risk, which appeals to small investors. FIs can also diversify far better than a household. (Slide note: the 1980s S&L debacle was tied to poor diversification in oil-state economies.)

Other special services

Reduced transaction costs
Economies of scale on trading.
Maturity intermediation
Funding long-term loans with short-term deposits and managing the mismatch.
Monetary policy transmission
Banks pass Fed actions through to the economy.
Credit allocation
Steering funds to areas of special need, like home mortgages, farms, small businesses.
Time intermediation
Intergenerational transfers: saving now for retirement or heirs (pensions, life insurers).
Payment services
Moving money: Fedwire and CHIPS. Protecting the payment system is a huge regulatory priority.
Denomination intermediation
Letting small savers own pieces of large assets (mutual funds).
Trigger words
  • "small saver, big asset, fund" = denomination
  • "long loans, short deposits" = maturity
  • "retirement, heirs, over a lifetime" = time intermediation
  • "housing, farms, special sectors" = credit allocation
  • "many savers let one institution watch the borrower" = delegated monitor
  • "Fedwire, CHIPS, wiring money" = payment services

Why FIs get special regulation

Because failure has negative externalities (it hurts people beyond the FI's owners), and because of their special services and institution-specific jobs: money supply transmission (banks), credit allocation (thrifts, farm banks), payment services (banks, thrifts).

An action that hurts a party outside the transaction. A bank failure wipes out savings, cuts firms off from credit, and can cause runs on sound banks (Ch 1 #21).
Charter value
Higher barriers to entry raise the profitability and charter value of existing FIs (Ch 1 #29).

Changing dynamics

  • Depository institutions' share of financial assets fell from 89.2% (1860) to 30.2% (2021). Investment companies rose to 31.1%. Insurance is 15.1%, pension funds 15.3%.
  • Possible reasons: the net regulatory burden on depository FIs, and savers preferring the denomination intermediation of mutual funds.
  • Enterprise risk management (ERM): managing risks as an interrelated portfolio. Before the crisis the focus was processes and systems; after, building a strong risk culture.
  • FinTech: technology that competes with traditional finance, like crypto and blockchain.
  • Global: only 2 of the top 10 banks are American; foreign banks usually hold over 10% of U.S. bank assets (as high as 21.9%).

CLEAREDcrisis

From the slides: Risk and the Financial Crisis (Ch 1 deck). Support: Appendix 1A

The financial crisis

The book calls it "the failure of financial services institution specialness": the things that make FIs special (monitoring, liquidity, transforming risk) broke down all at once.

Why it happened

  • Subprime lending: homeowners stretched to buy or refinance in the early 2000s. Home prices fell starting late 2006 and delinquencies surged.
  • Securitization: banks packaged mortgages into mortgage-backed securities and sold them everywhere, so the losses spread through the whole system instead of staying with the lender. The book calls securitized subprime loans possibly the root cause.
  • Originate and distribute: after the 1999 FSM Act, lenders shifted from "originate and hold" to "originate and distribute," which weakened the incentive to screen and monitor borrowers (Ch 1 slide).
  • Off-balance-sheet risk and a housing bubble encouraged more exotic mortgages.
  • Confidence collapse: interbank trust broke in August 2007 and never fully recovered, so LIBOR spiked and credit froze.

Scale: the Dow fell 53.8% in under 18 months (worse than the 49% drop of 1937 to 1938). 1 in 45 households was in mortgage default in 2009. Unemployment topped 10% in October 2009, the highest since 1983.

Dodd-Frank's five objectives

  1. Supervise financial firms: a new Financial Services Oversight Council (chaired by Treasury), Fed supervision of any firm that threatens stability even without a bank, higher capital standards, a National Bank supervisor, closing the thrift charter loophole, hedge fund advisors register with the SEC.
  2. Supervise financial markets: regulate securitization (issuers keep some skin in the game), tighter rules on rating agencies, regulate OTC derivatives, Fed oversight of payment and clearing systems.
  3. Protect consumers and investors: a Consumer Financial Protection agency (became the CFPB).
  4. Give government crisis tools: a resolution regime for failing nonbanks, more accountability for Fed emergency lending.
  5. Raise international standards and cooperation.

Monetary policy tools

The Fed steers the economy by pushing bank reserves up or down. Everything else (rates, money supply, lending, exchange rates, prices) follows from that.

The setup

Reserves
Required plus excess reserves: deposits at the Fed plus vault cash.
Fed funds rate
The rate banks charge each other to borrow excess reserves, usually overnight. It's the benchmark rate the Fed uses to guide policy.
FOMC
Federal Open Market Committee. Sets the target and sends a policy directive to the Trading Desk at the New York Fed (FRBNY).
Monetary base
Currency in circulation plus reserves.
Two approaches
Target the quantity of reserves (money supply) or target the price of reserves (fed funds rate). Since 1993 it's been the rate.

Tool 1: open market operations (the workhorse)

The Fed buys or sells Treasury securities with primary dealers. Each morning the FRBNY staff reviews the fed funds market and yesterday's reserves, forecasts today's reserve supply and demand, and holds a conference call with the Board's Monetary Affairs Division and one of the four voting (non-New York) Reserve Bank presidents. Then the Desk trades.

  • Buy securities: the Fed credits the seller's bank's reserve account, so reserves go up, banks can lend more.
  • Sell securities: dealers pay from their bank accounts, so reserves go down.

Treasuries are used because the market is deep and liquid enough to absorb huge trades without big price swings.

Tool 2: the discount rate

The rate the Fed charges banks that borrow directly at the discount window. Its real job is as a signal of where the FOMC wants rates to go. Raising it signals tightening; cutting it signals easing.

Why it's rarely used as a policy lever: (1) the Fed can't predict how much banks will actually borrow when the rate changes, so the effect on the money supply is uncertain, and (2) the signal is so loud it can jolt markets. Example: Powell's November 27, 2018 comments suggesting a pause in rate hikes sent the Dow up 617.70 points.

The January 2003 overhaul

Before 2003, the window rate was set below the fed funds rate and only for emergencies, and banks had to prove they couldn't borrow elsewhere, which created a stigma. After 2003: higher cost, easier terms, any sound bank can borrow.

Tool 3: reserve requirements

The minimum share of transaction deposits a bank must hold as reserves (vault cash plus deposits at the Fed), say 10%. Cut the ratio and banks have excess reserves to lend. That money gets spent, redeposited, and lent again, so deposits grow by a multiple.

Δ Deposits = (1 / new RR) x Δ Reserves
With a cash drain: Δ Deposits = [1 / (RR + c)] x Δ Reserves
c = the public's cash-to-deposit ratio. The multiplier also works for open market operations.
Example 1B-3: City Bank has $400m of transaction deposits and RR is 10%. The Fed cuts RR to 5%.
Show the answer
  1. Required reserves fall from $40m (0.10 x 400) to $20m (0.05 x 400), so $20m becomes excess.
  2. First round: lend $20m, it's redeposited, deposits hit $420m. Now $1m more is required, $19m is still excess.
  3. Final: Δ deposits = (1/0.05) x $20m = $400m, so deposits double to $800m (and 0.05 x 800 = the $40m of reserves the bank already had).

Balance sheet at the end: loans $760m, reserves $40m, deposits $800m.

Show the answer

Show the answer

Trap: the multiplier is a maximum

The textbook result assumes one bank, 100% of excess reserves lent, 100% spent, and 100% redeposited. Relax any of those and the real effect is smaller and less certain. That unpredictability is exactly why the Fed rarely uses reserve requirements. (Real-world aside: the Fed cut reserve requirements to zero in March 2020, but answer exam questions from the book.)

Expansionary vs contractionary

Impact onExpansionary (buy securities, cut discount rate, cut RR)Contractionary (sell securities, raise discount rate, raise RR)

The chain for expansionary policy: lower rates, more borrowing, more spending on housing, plants, cars, and public projects, a weaker dollar (lower U.S. rates vs foreign rates), cheaper U.S. goods, more exports. The goal is real growth without inflation. Price stability is the Fed's primary objective.

Memory trick

Rates and bond prices always move opposite. If the Fed is "adding," everything goes up except rates.

Money supply vs interest rate targeting

The Fed can hit only one target at a time. Target the money supply and rates bounce around as money demand shifts (Panel A: rates swing between 8% and 4% around a 6% target). Target the rate and the money supply has to flex to hold it (Panel B: if demand falls, the Fed sells securities to shrink supply and hold 6%).

Depository institutions

Three groups: commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions. The exam cares most about commercial banks.

Blurring lines

In 1950, each type of FI sold a narrow set of products. By 2019, almost everyone sells almost everything: depository institutions now underwrite debt and equity and sell insurance and risk management products. What makes depositories special is that they sell products on both sides of the balance sheet: loans (assets) and deposits (liabilities).

Consolidation

  • Commercial banks: 14,416 in 1985, 12,744 in 1989, 4,774 in 2018, 4,231 in 2021. Mostly from mergers and acquisitions, which were restricted before the 1980s and 1990s.
  • Drivers: disintermediation, global competition, regulatory change, technology, and competition across FI types.
  • Asset concentration: in 1984, the 24 banks over $10 billion (0.2% of banks) held 34.5% of assets. In 2021, 142 banks over $10 billion (3.4%) held 86.9%. The smallest banks' share keeps shrinking.
  • Structural change usually comes from regulatory change. Example: Riegle-Neal (1994) allowed interstate branching.
  • Limited powers to underwrite corporate securities existed only since 1987; the 1999 FSM Act let banks, investment banks, and insurers merge.

Balance sheet trends since 1987

Business (C&I) loans have declined in importance while securities and mortgages have grown. Why:

  • Big firms can borrow directly in the commercial paper market instead of from banks.
  • Securitization of mortgages.
  • Temporary effects: credit crunches in the 1989 to 1992 and 2001 to 2002 recessions.

Primary liabilities (Sept 30, 2018): deposits $12,631.6B, borrowings $1,589.4B, other liabilities $390.3B. The inference: maturity mismatch (interest rate risk) and liquidity risk are the key exposures.

Savings institutions and credit unions

The slide lists them as the other two depository groups. The Ch 2 questions cover the details:

  • Savings institutions (thrifts) hold mostly mortgages and MBS, so they carry a lot of interest rate risk and little diversification. The QTL test requires 65% of assets in mortgage-related assets to keep the charter. Savings banks can hold more corporate stocks and bonds than savings associations.
  • Why thrifts failed in the 1980s: the Fed's October 1979 to 1982 tight money sent rates (T-bills up to 16%) above what thrifts earned on fixed-rate mortgages, and Regulation Q capped what they could pay depositors. DIDMCA (1980) and Garn-St Germain (1982) expanded their powers, but the FSLIC didn't close insolvent thrifts and became insolvent itself. FIRREA (1989) cleaned it up.
  • Credit unions are nonprofit mutuals whose members share a common bond (job, association, or community). Their income isn't taxed and they aren't subject to the CRA, so they can pay more on deposits and charge less on some loans. They have virtually no borrowings.

Report of condition

Report of income
The income statement. Revenues, expenses, and net profit over a period of time.
Filed
Quarterly (March, June, September, December), to regulators and stockholders. The FFIEC sets the uniform forms.

Slide example: the $10 million loan commitment (Figure 2B-1)

From your notes: normal company vs bank

A normal company goes Revenue − COGS − SG&A = EBIT, then interest (to debt holders) and taxes (to government), leaving NI for equity holders. Provision vs allowance vs write-off

This trips people up every semester, so here's the one-sentence version: the provision is the expense on the income statement, the allowance is the running reserve on the balance sheet, and a write-off just uses up the allowance.

EventGross loansAllowanceNet loansNet income and equity
Record a provisionNo changeUpDownDown (it's an expense)
Write off a bad loanDownDownNo changeNo change
Recover a loan you wrote offNo changeUp (added back)n/aNo change

Net write-offs = actual loan losses minus recoveries.

Book example 2B-1

A bank starts the month with $1,000,000 of gross loans, a $50,000 allowance, $300,000 retained earnings, and $500,000 total equity. Management estimates $5,000 more won't be repaid. A month later it writes the $5,000 loan off. What happens at each step?

Show the answer

End of month 1: provision of $5,000. Allowance rises to $55,000, net loans fall to $945,000, retained earnings fall to $295,000, total equity to $495,000, total assets to $1,195,000. The loan is still on the books.

End of month 2: write-off. Gross loans fall to $995,000 and the allowance falls back to $50,000, so net loans stay at $945,000. No income or equity effect, because the hit already happened when the provision was booked.

The CECL reform: during the crisis, provisions were "too little, too late," because banks only recorded one once a loss showed signs of happening. FASB approved a rule in June 2016 requiring banks to look ahead and record expected losses on the day a loan is made. Public banks adopted it in 2020, private banks in 2021. Critics say it makes earnings more volatile.

Real example: in Q2 2009, Bank of America added $13.4 billion to its loan loss reserve and its earnings per share fell 54%.

The ROE framework

ROE tells you how profitable a bank is. Breaking it apart tells you why, and that "why" is what the exam grades.

The chain

ROE = ROA × EM = PM × AU × EM
NIE = NIA × AE = NITOI × TOIA × AE
Cancel the TOIs and the A's and you're left with NI/E. If your numbers don't multiply back to ROE, you have an error somewhere.
RatioFormulaWhat it tells you
ROENet income / Total equityProfit per dollar the shareholders put in
ROANet income / Total assetsProfit per dollar of assets
EM (equity multiplier)Total assets / Total equityLeverage. Higher EM = more debt funding, more solvency risk
PM (profit margin)Net income / Total operating incomeExpense control: how much of each revenue dollar survives to the bottom line
AU (asset utilization)Total operating income / Total assetsHow much revenue the assets generate
Trap: a higher ROE is not automatically good
  • ROE rises if equity falls. That can mean the bank is closer to violating capital rules and has more insolvency risk.
  • PM rises if salaries drop, but maybe because the best people left ("labor quality" problem).
  • AU and NIM rise if the bank swaps safe loans for risky high-yield ones. Returns look better right up until the defaults hit.

On the exam, whenever a ratio improves, ask what caused it.

Trap: EM and the equity ratio are flips of each other

EM = 1 / (equity/assets). An equity ratio of 8% means EM = 12.5. An EM of 10 means equity is 10% of assets. The book converts Heartland's EM of 9.49x into an equity ratio of 10.53%.

Profit margin broken down

Every dollar of total operating income gets eaten by four expenses. Whatever's left is PM.

PM = 1 − ER − PR − NIER − TR
ER = Interest expense / TOI   (ratio 9)
PR = Provision for loan losses / TOI   (ratio 10)
NIER = Noninterest expense / TOI   (ratio 11)
TR = Income taxes / TOI   (ratio 12)
From your notes
  • Loan loss provision: a good way to check whether a bank is really more successful. Look at the last few years of statements to see if a high provision is an off year or a consistent pattern. (Heartland's was slightly negative in 2016, and the whole industry's was negative in 2021.)
  • Tax ratio: what percentage of revenue (TOI) the bank pays in taxes. Not the same as the tax rate on pretax income.
  • Your notes call the interest expense ratio ER or IER; the book calls it ratio 9. Same thing.

Lower is better for each one, but a suspiciously low ratio can hide a problem. You can drill further: interest expense on each type of liability over TOI (ratios 13 to 20), liability yields (24 to 31), each liability as a share of total assets (32 to 44).

Asset utilization broken down

AU = Interest income + Noninterest incomeTotal assets
AU = Interest income ratio + Noninterest income ratio
Interest income ratio = Interest income / Total assets  (ratio 60)
Noninterest income ratio = Noninterest income / Total assets  (ratio 61)

These aren't independent: making more loans raises interest income and fee income. Further breakdowns use asset yields (income on C&I loans / C&I loans), asset mix (each asset / total assets), and OBS notional amounts / total assets.

Three other ratios

NIM = (Interest income − Interest expense) / Earning assets
Spread = Interest income / Earning assets − Interest expense / Interest-bearing liabilities
Overhead efficiency = Noninterest income / Noninterest expense
Earning assets = investment securities + net loans and leases
Trap: watch the denominators

NIM uses earning assets, not total assets. The spread uses two different denominators: earning assets for the yield, interest-bearing liabilities for the cost. Overhead efficiency is almost always under 100%, because noninterest expense (the burden) is usually bigger than noninterest income. Very low overhead can also be a risk flag if the bank is skimping on technology or back office.

Niche and size effects

  • Size, the usual pattern: big banks hold less equity (easy access to capital markets), use more purchased funds and fewer core deposits, spend more on salaries and premises, generate more noninterest income (trading, derivatives, FX), and have far more OBS activity. BOA's OBS notional was 1,659.10% of its assets vs 26.16% for Heartland.
  • But bigger isn't more profitable: a St. Louis Fed study found ROA rises with size up to $15 billion in assets, then falls.
  • In this example BOA broke the pattern twice: more equity than Heartland and a lower ROE.
Ch 2 question 2: why small banks have higher ROA but big banks have higher ROE

Small banks earn wider spreads in less competitive local markets, control credit risk well, and run with less overhead, so their ROA is higher. Big banks run with less equity per dollar of assets (higher EM), so their ROE is higher. You can back out the equity ratio as ROA / ROE: in 2018, $100 million to $1 billion banks had ROE 11.33% and ROA 1.25%, so equity was 11.03% of assets (EM 9.06).

Time-series vs cross-sectional

Time-series analysis compares one bank's ratios over time. Cross-sectional analysis compares a bank to its peers, using tools like the Uniform Bank Performance Report (UBPR) from the FFIEC. Picking peers is subjective because no two banks are identical. Big banks also use RAROC (risk-adjusted return on capital) because ROE ignores lending risk.

Trap: "Glass-Steagall" vs "Banking Act of 1933"

Same law for exam purposes. Non-bank financial institutions

Learn the definitions, the types, and the handful of famous examples from the slides.

Finance companies

  • Lend like banks but take no deposits. Attractive rates on some loans (autos), willing to lend to riskier borrowers, often tied to a manufacturer, lightly regulated.
  • History: first major one came out of the Great Depression (installment credit, GE Capital, now GECGH). Bank competition grew in the 1950s.
  • GMAC: the Fed controversially approved it as a bank holding company in December 2008, giving it access to $6 billion of bailout money. GM had to cut its stake from 49% to under 10%.
TypeLends toExamples

Personal creditIndividualsHSBC Finance, AIG American General, Synchrony
Business creditBusinesses, often via equipment leasing and factoringCIT Group, U.S. Bancorp Equipment Finance, IBM Global Financing

Securities firms and investment banks

  • Securities firms: buy, sell, and broker existing securities (retail side).
  • Investment banks: originate, underwrite, and place new securities; advise on M&A and restructuring (commercial side).
  • Bulge-bracket firms do both underwriting and brokerage. Boutique firms specialize in originating, underwriting, and distributing new issues.
  • Crisis: JPMorgan bought Bear Stearns, Lehman went bankrupt, BofA bought Merrill. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley were the only majors left and became bank holding companies in 2008.
  • Firm types: national full-line firms are largest (BofA via Merrill, Morgan Stanley); full-line firms focused on corporate finance are second (Goldman); then regional firms, discount brokers, electronic trading, venture capital, and others.
  • Key activities: investment banking (IPOs and seasoned issues, public and private), venture capital, market making, trading (position, pure arbitrage, risk arbitrage, program trading, brokerage), investing, cash management, M&A, back office.

Mutual funds and hedge funds

  • Mutual funds give small investors diversification and economies of scale. Mostly open-end: shares outstanding go up and down every day. Closed-end funds have a fixed number of shares and can trade at a premium or discount (REITs are an example).
  • Institutional funds hold 80% of retirement plan investments: low costs, no extra distribution fees, risk levels set by the plan sponsor. Low barriers to entry but highly concentrated.
  • Hedge funds are not technically mutual funds and are exempt from many investor-protection rules. Not SEC regulated before 2010. Examples of trouble: Madoff, the Bear Stearns High Grade fund, and LTCM (near collapse, $3.6 billion bailout, which triggered SEC scrutiny).
  • Hedge fund types: more risky (market directional), moderate risk (market neutral or value), risk-avoidance (market neutral, steady low-risk returns). Fees: management fees plus performance fees.

Insurance companies

  • Life insurers: about 740 in 2018 with $7 trillion of assets (vs 2,300+ with $1.1 trillion in 1988). Top 10 wrote 32.7% of new business. Biggest: Prudential ($577.9B).
  • Adverse selection: the people who buy insurance are riskier than average (Matt buys life insurance right after a terminal diagnosis). Fixed by grouping policyholders into similar risk pools. Hits both life and P&C.
  • Life products: ordinary life (term, whole, endowment; newer variable, universal, variable universal), group, industrial, credit life. Plus annuities (the reverse of life insurance, $292.2B in 2017), private pensions, and accident and health (morbidity risk).
  • P&C: about 2,620 firms; top 10 have 51% of premiums, top 200 over 95%. Products: fire, homeowners multiple-peril, commercial multiple-peril, auto liability and physical damage, other liability.
